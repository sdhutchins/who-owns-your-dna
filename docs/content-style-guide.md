# Content style guide

## Purpose

Who Owns Your DNA? helps people find and understand U.S. laws and policies
that affect genetic information and biological samples. The writing should
help a reader identify what a record covers, what it does, where its limits
are, and how to verify it.

Plain language does not mean removing legal detail. It means presenting the
most important information first, defining necessary terms, and preserving
the scope and uncertainty of the primary source.

## Audiences

Write for readers who may understand genetics, law, both, or neither. Do not
assume that a reader knows legislative procedure, insurance terminology,
research governance, or the differences among privacy, confidentiality,
security, and control.

## Content layers

Keep these layers visibly separate:

1. **Factual summary:** Identifies the record and its overall subject without
   interpretation.
2. **Legal effects:** States what the law or policy requires, allows, or
   prohibits and identifies the relevant actor.
3. **Plain-language explanation:** Helps a reader understand the practical
   subject of the record without expanding its legal scope.
4. **Important limits and exceptions:** States exclusions, unresolved scope,
   incomplete coverage, and other reasons the short explanation may not apply
   to a particular situation.
5. **Advocacy context:** Explains why advocates are watching an issue. This is
   optional and must not be presented as the legal effect of the record.
6. **Primary source:** Provides the official text that supports the record.

## Record questions

Every project-authored record should help the reader answer:

1. What is this law or policy?
2. Is it in force, proposed, or no longer active?
3. Who must follow it?
4. What does it require, allow, or prohibit?
5. What does it not cover, or what exceptions may apply?
6. When was the source checked, and where is the official text?

If the primary source does not answer one of these questions, state that the
answer was not identified. Do not infer an answer from silence.

## Sentence-level guidance

- Put the actor before the action: "Covered employers may not use..."
- Prefer concrete verbs such as `requires`, `allows`, `limits`, `prohibits`,
  `applies`, and `exempts`.
- Use one main claim per sentence.
- Introduce the familiar term before the technical term when both are needed.
- Define an abbreviation the first time it appears on a page.
- Replace vague references such as "specified uses" with the uses themselves
  when a concise list is supported by the source.
- Explain what words such as `covered`, `eligible`, `authorized`, and
  `deidentified` refer to in the record.
- Use active voice when the responsible actor is known.
- Keep dates and legal status explicit.
- Avoid promotional, persuasive, or alarmist wording.

## Scope and uncertainty

Place material limits next to the explanation they qualify. Do not hide them
in technical metadata or rely only on a site-wide disclaimer.

Use:

- "The project has not identified a specific law in the reviewed sources."
- "Coverage is still expanding."
- "The law contains exceptions that may apply to a particular use."
- "Review the current primary source before relying on this status."

Do not use:

- "No law exists" when the dataset is incomplete.
- "This protects your DNA" without identifying the actor, conduct, and scope.
- "Anonymous" as a synonym for `deidentified` unless the source does so and
  the distinction is explained.
- A proposed bill's requirements in the present tense without stating that the
  bill did not become law.

## Source archive boundary

NHGRI source summaries are archival text. Preserve their wording, including
historical terminology and imperfections. Label them as retrieved source text.
If the project adds a clearer explanation, store and display it separately as
project-authored language. Never silently replace the archived wording.

## Review process

Before publishing project-authored language:

1. Compare each claim with the primary source.
2. Confirm that the actor, action, subject, status, and limitations agree with
   the source.
3. Check that the plain-language explanation does not broaden the legal effect.
4. Ask a reader to explain the record in their own words.
5. Check whether the reader can identify who is covered, what the record does,
   an important limitation, its status, and the primary source.
6. Revise confusing language and repeat the review.

Readability scores may be recorded as a diagnostic, but they do not replace
reader testing or expert source review.

## Evidence informing this guide

- van der Giessen JA, et al. [Development of a plain-language guide for
  discussing breast cancer genetic counseling and testing with patients with
  limited health literacy](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00520-020-05800-7).
- Brett GR, et al. [Co-design, implementation, and evaluation of plain language
  genomic test reports](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41525-022-00332-x).
- Ayre J, et al. [Online Plain Language Tool and Health Information
  Quality](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2024.37955).
- Zheng J, Yu H. [Readability Formulas and User Perceptions of Electronic
  Health Records Difficulty](https://doi.org/10.2196/jmir.6962).
- Clayton EW, et al. [A systematic literature review of individuals'
  perspectives on privacy and genetic information in the United
  States](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0204417).
- Kerr JR, et al. [Transparent communication of evidence does not undermine
  public trust in evidence](https://doi.org/10.1093/pnasnexus/pgac280).
- Prince AER, et al. [The goldilocks conundrum: Disclosing discrimination risks
  in informed consent](https://doi.org/10.1002/jgc4.1613).

These studies concern health and genomic communication rather than legal-policy
trackers specifically. Their findings guide this project's writing process,
but improvements must be evaluated with this site's intended readers.
